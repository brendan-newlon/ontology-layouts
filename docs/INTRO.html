Ontology diagrams a reader can follow

The difference on one ontology

A customer-data ontology of 31 classes around one large hub, drawn by Graphviz neato, a widely used layout, and by ours (Best Read with its clean-up). Dots mark crossed links; red links run through a class they do not belong to, so two unrelated classes look connected. Links are drawn straight here; in use, ours also bends the few links that remain.

Both drawings are recorded in the benchmarks (the open-source layouts at general settings there; tuned, they do somewhat better). Every test graph can be compared in the Benchmarks.

How it works

  1. Placement. Two layouts place every class at once so that distance on the page follows distance in the graph: Tidy Stress for typical ontologies, Mycelium for large and hub-heavy ones, which keeps closely linked groups together. Best Read runs both and keeps the more readable drawing.
  2. Clean-up, taught by a person. Then the drawing is tidied the way a person tidies it: a class moved off a link into open space, leaves turned round the class they hang from, crowded corners spread out. These moves were learned from recorded sessions of a person tidying drawings by hand in the Lab, distilled into rules with an AI assistant.
  3. Link routing. Parallel links become arcs side by side, self-links loops, a link that still runs through a class bends round it, and link names go where they cover nothing.

Each step can be watched in the Playback, switched off to see what it is worth, or replaced by your own layout: the clean-up and the routing also tidy a drawing made by another layout. The same methods keep a drawing stable as a graph grows: expand a class, and nothing already drawn moves.

How it was built

The placement stands on published methods: stress majorization started from pivot MDS, Louvain community detection to find groups of closely linked classes, and readability measures from graph-drawing research. The clean-up did not come from the literature. Brendan Newlon tidied drawings by hand in the Lab while saying why. An AI assistant, Claude, distilled his reasons into rules, and every rule was kept only if it held on graphs nobody had looked at. Then he watched the result in the Playback and named what was still wrong in a few words ("don't fence me in", "pull it in from the edge"); each phrase became a short pass of its own. Ideas that did not hold up, such as bending links instead of moving classes, were measured and dropped. The full account, with the teaching sessions and the lab to try, is on How it was built.

The Lab and the Playback are offered as tools in their own right, for any problem a person can see and fix better than they can describe: how the method works.
