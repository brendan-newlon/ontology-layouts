Graph layout for ontologies

Tidy Stress and Mycelium

Two layouts that draw ontologies with about half the crossed links of the widely used open-source layouts, even when those are tuned, and an auto mode, Best Read, that picks the better of the two for a given ontology. A clean-up step, distilled from how a person explained and demonstrated tidying these drawings, then takes most of the remaining links off the classes they run through.

An ontology diagram is only useful if a reader can follow each link from one class to another. Two things make that hard: links that cross, and links that pass under a class box, so that two unrelated classes look connected. General-purpose layouts reduce the first only as a side effect and do little about the second. Tidy Stress and Mycelium are built to minimize both, without letting the drawing sprawl.

What we value, and what we deliberately do not. These layouts are for ontologies, not for graphs in general, and that decides which measures count. All rankings on this page use these five:

  • Crossed links and links through classes come first. Each can make a reader follow the wrong link.
  • Crossing angle and angular resolution come next. A shallow crossing is harder to follow than a square one, and links that leave a class in almost the same direction are hard to tell apart.
  • Area matters, but less than all four.

Two common measures are left out on purpose: how faithfully drawn distance follows graph distance (normalized stress), and how even the link lengths are. A business ontology often joins several domains, such as sales, finance and operations, each linked back to shared classes like Person or Organization. Drawing every related class close would pull the domains into each other; keeping each domain together, even when that makes a link to a shared class long, is the point. Length and distance are therefore not goals here, and the main rankings leave them out. Readers who value them, or a top-down hierarchy, can add them with the tabs above each ranking table; our layouts do poorly on distance and length. Measured over the 51 graphs: normalized stress is 0.085 after the stress placement alone, 0.132 after Tidy Stress's search, 0.146 for Best Read and 0.159 after the clean-up and the routing. Global distances are therefore about half as faithful. Local closeness is not lost: the share of a class's linked classes that are among its nearest classes is 55.8% after the stress placement and 55.6% at the end. A reader can still take nearness as linked; they should not read a long distance as unrelated.

The layouts

Tidy Stress up to a few dozen classes

Places classes so that straight-line distances match how many links apart they are, then tidies the drawing: it moves classes to remove crossings, to take them off links that run through them, and to shorten long links, keeping a move only if nothing else gets worse.

Mycelium large and hub-heavy ontologies

Draws groups of closely linked classes as compact pods joined by longer threads. Every hub gets a ring wide enough for all its neighbors, and classes with a single link fan out around the class they hang from, with room for the link's label.

Tree taxonomies, on request

Not used by Best Read since 2026-11-07; offered on its own. Draws subclasses below their superclasses. Classes stand on levels, each one level below its deepest superclass, so every superclass is above its subclasses, second superclasses included. Subtrees sit side by side under their parent, as in a tidy tree (Reingold and Tilford, 1981), and each level sits deep enough below the one above that no line from a parent to a child can clip a sibling. The tree itself therefore has no crossing and no link through a class. Our own parts are the levels by the deepest superclass and that depth rule.

Link routing after any layout

Draws the links on the final positions: parallel and reciprocal links as arcs side by side, self-links as loops on the most open side, links still running through a class bent round it inside the drawing, link ends spread along the side of a crowded class, and link names placed on or beside their link where they cover nothing; a class that a link still runs through is pushed off it when that helps. See Link routing.

Clean-up after any layout

Fixes what is left the way a person does: moves a class off a link into nearby open space, turns leaves and small groups that hang from one class until their links are clear, and spreads the regions where problems pile up. It uses what is left of 1.5 s, at most half a second. See Clean-up after the layout.

Auto mode: Best Read

Best Read chooses between two layouts for a given ontology: it runs Tidy Stress and Mycelium, scores both drawings for readability, keeps the better one, and cleans it up. (Until 2026-11-07 it also drew the Tree when at least half the links were subclass links and kept it when that cost nothing; over the 51 benchmark graphs that happened once, on Dublin Core terms, where the other drawing has no problem either, so it was dropped: these layouts are for business models joined mostly by relationships.) Above 100 classes it runs Mycelium alone: at that size Mycelium drew far fewer crossings and links through classes in every test, and running both would double the time. It changes no setting inside any layout; it only chooses between their results, by crossed links, links through classes, near misses, overlaps, mean link length and area. The Tree is also offered on its own, for any model where the hierarchy matters more than the last few crossings (see Experiments).

Clean-up after the layout

Both layouts place classes by a global rule: stress, pods, hub rings. What a global rule leaves behind is local: a leaf sitting on a line, a corner where five links cross, a class that could step two box-widths aside into empty space. A person sees these at a glance and fixes them in a minute. The clean-up does the same automatically, and it was taught by a person who explained how they do it (see How the clean-up was learned).

Over the 15 test graphs, before and after the clean-up (totals; area is the drawing's bounding box divided by the total area of the class boxes). In each cell the value before the arrow is without the clean-up and the bold value after it is with the clean-up. Crossed links, links through classes, area and time are lower-is-better, so a lower value right of the arrow means the clean-up improved the drawing; the two angle measures are higher-is-better.

Links through classes fall by two thirds (Best Read 124 → 39). Crossings fall a little (Best Read draws 7% fewer), crossing angles get slightly squarer, and since 2026-11-08 the drawing gets smaller too (Best Read's area 8.0 → 6.3 times its boxes, from pulling sprawling parts and groups in from the edge). The cost is time: Best Read's median rises from 0.2 to 0.5 s. The clean-up takes whatever is left of 1.5 s after the layout, at most half a second, and is skipped when less than 0.1 s is left. Above 100 classes the layout is given 0.6 s so that the clean-up keeps its half second: on large graphs that gives fewer problems than a longer layout. To fit in that time, the clean-up looks only at the links and classes near each move, through a grid of cells one link long, with the same results as checking every pair.

After the clean-up: three ideas from watching it work

Watching the clean-up step by step in the Layout Playback, a person named what was still wrong in a few words. Each phrase became a short pass that runs after the clean-up, and each pass keeps a move only where it measures better:

  • "Don't fence me in." A one-link class whose link still crosses another link or runs through a class looks round its neighbor (48 directions, out to about four link lengths) for the spot with the fewest problems, then the most room, then the shortest link. The math: candidate c scores 200·x(c) − room(c) + 0.2·r, where x counts 6 per crossing and 10 per link through a class (9 px margin), room is the clearance to the nearest box or link (capped at 120 px) and r the distance to the neighbor; taken only if x falls.
  • "Push it off the line." A class that a link runs through, or passes within 9 px of, moves sideways off the line just far enough (or twice that), or a one-link end turns round its neighbor by 8 to 30 degrees; kept only if the problems involving the class fall.
  • "Pull it in from the edge." A chain or small tree hanging by one link that sprawls into empty canvas could fold up or down instead, and groups with an empty middle between them could sit closer. So a part that hangs off the rest by one link (a one-link class, a chain, a small tree, up to 40% of the classes) turns round the class it hangs from (up to 180 degrees) and/or draws in toward it (to 85, 70 or 55% of its reach), and whole groups (Louvain communities of four or more) slide toward the middle of the drawing in one of 16 directions by 40 to 640 px. The math: a move is taken only if the problems involving the part do not rise (6 per crossing, 10 per link through a class), no box comes within 14 px of another (40 px between groups), no drawn-in link leaves less than 70 px between its two boxes (room for its name), and either the drawing's bounding box shrinks by more than 1% or, with the box unchanged, the part's links get at least 25% shorter. Parts nearest the edge go first; at most 100 ms of the work clock for parts and 200 ms in all. A group may also move inward when the box stays the same, so that when two groups hold one edge, the second move can shrink it.

A worked example

A 115-class biomedical taxonomy, drawn by Best Read, then cleaned up. The steps follow the order a person works in: look at the whole drawing, find the worst region, make room there, then make the easy fixes. Each step has two tabs: the reasoning in plain terms, and the same step as the code computes it.

Before fixing anything, look at the whole drawing with an imaginary grid over it. Where is the drawing, and where is the empty canvas inside its rectangle? Open space (green) is a resource: it is where classes can go to solve problems.

Then look at where the problems are: red dots are crossings, red links run through a class. They are not spread evenly. They cluster where the drawing is crowded, and one crowded spot often causes several at once: a leaf with no room near its parent ends up further away, crossing links on the way.

Let L̃ be the median link length. The open-space grid has cells of side 0.3 L̃ (here 45 px) over the bounding box. A cell is open when no class box, grown by a clearance c = 8 px, overlaps it and no link passes through it (links are sampled every third of a cell).

A crossing is a pair of links without a shared end whose straight segments intersect (orientation test). A link through a class is a link uv whose segment meets the box of a third class w grown by c (Liang–Barsky clipping). A class is cramped when the smallest angle between its links is under 15°; a crossing is sharp under 30°.

Count the problems in each cell of a coarser grid, one link long. The cells with the most problems (orange) form the region most in need of fixing; its center of gravity is the mean position of its problems.

Around that center, cut the space into eight pizza slices (dashed). Each slice is judged by how much open or sparse space lies just beyond the crowd in its direction (the shading): that is where there is room to push.

A crowded part of the drawing with no problem in it is left alone. Dense is fine when it reads well.

Problem points: the crossing point of a crossing, the center of the class a link runs through. On cells of side L̃, n(cell) counts problem points. Hot cells have n ≥ max(2, ⌈max n / 2⌉); regions are 8-connected groups of hot cells, ranked by Σn.

Center c = mean of the region's problem points; radius r₀ = min(1.5 L̃, maxi |pi − c| + 0.6 L̃). Slice k = 0…7 covers angles [−π + kπ/4, −π + (k+1)π/4).

Density ρ on cells of side L̃/2: 1 per class center and ¼ per link passing through, mean ρ̄. Room in slice k: Σ over cells at distance r₀ … r₀ + 2L̃ of max(0, ρ̄ − ρ) + ½·[ρ = 0].

Move every class in the region away from the center: further for classes near the region's edge, and further in slices with more room beyond them. Classes just outside are pushed too, less the further out they are, so the push makes room without piling a new crowd at the edge.

The push alone fixes nothing here: the counts are unchanged. It only makes room, which is why it is never judged on its own.

For a class at p in slice k with d = |p − c| < 3r₀:

p′ = p + a · L̃ · wk · f(d) · (p − c) / d

with f(d) = d / r₀ inside the region and max(0, 1 − (d − r₀) / 2r₀) beyond it, and wk = 1 (even) or 0.3 + 0.7 · roomk / max room (weighted by room). Six candidates, a ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 0.8} × the two weightings; the one with the lowest cost goes on to the repair.

With room made, a few single moves repair the region: a class at the end of a crossed link, or one a link runs through, moves into nearby open space. The spread is kept only if, after these repairs, the drawing is better than before it; otherwise everything goes back.

Then the easy fixes. A leaf, a short chain of leaves or a leaf triangle hangs off one class, so it can always be turned around that class until its links are clear. A leaf left on a line is a sign that a step was missed, not that the drawing is hard. A long label goes beside its neighbor rather than above or below, because a long label reads best on a link that runs sideways.

Here the repairs after the spread take links through classes from 10 to 7, remove the sharp crossing and add one crossing. The rest of the clean-up (three smaller spreads, three single moves and four leaf units) takes links through classes to 1. The figure shows the end, with arrows from the drawing right after the push.

Cost F = 6·crossings + 3·sharp crossings + 10·links through classes + 1·cramped classes + 1000·overlapping boxes + 600·max(0, A/A₀ − 1), with A the bounding-box area and A₀ its value before the clean-up. Link length has no term. A move is costed locally, over the links and classes it touches.

Repair: up to 4 greedy single moves among the classes in problems within 1.5r₀ + L̃ of c, each trying 16 nudges (8 directions × 0.1 and 0.25 L̃) and the 12 nearest open cells within 1.8 L̃. The spread is kept when F falls by more than 0.5.

Leaf units: for each class a, the parts of the graph cut off by removing a (a cut vertex) with at most 4 classes. Candidates: 24 turns about a × mirror × orderings (up to 3 classes); a single leaf also at 1, 1.6 and 2.4 times its distance, sideways directions first when its label is over 100 px wide; then sizes 0.75–2.8 at ±7.5°. A move is kept when F falls.

All the steps, and what each adds

In the layouts the clean-up runs in rounds, each round in this order, until nothing improves or the time is up:

  1. Single moves: each class in a problem tries nearby open cells and small nudges.
  2. Leaf units turned around their anchor.
  3. Region spreading, as in the example, for the six regions with the most problems.
  4. Group moves: for each problem left, the classes on one side of the problem line, picked by position rather than by their links, move together away from it, with up to 4 repair moves; kept only if the drawing ends up better.

A last pass over leaf units and single classes follows, because the group moves can leave a leaf on a line. A class or problem that could not be improved is tried again only once something near it has moved.

The example spreads first, the way a person works. In the layouts, spreading comes after the single moves and leaf units, because that order measured better. The 15 graphs, cleaned up from the same starting drawings with half a second each; they start with 138 links through classes:

How the clean-up was learned

The clean-up was not designed from the literature, and it does not replay anyone's moves. A person who works with these ontologies taught it to an AI coding assistant (Claude): they solved the drawings by hand while saying what they were doing and why, named the principle behind each move, often with a metaphor (open space as a resource, a crowd pushed outward in pizza slices, a leaf that can always be solved), and suggested how a program could recognize the situation and carry the move out. The assistant's part was to distill that intuition into definitions, measures and steps a program can run, and to test whether each one held up on every graph, not only the one it was shown on. The loop had four parts.

  1. Showing and telling. In the Layout Lab the person tidied drawings by hand, typing what they were about to do and why before each move. The point was never the moves themselves, which only fit the graph they were made on, but the reasoning: what the person looked at first, what they treated as a problem and what as harmless, which move they reached for and when they gave up on one. Within about ten minutes per ontology, this observed problem solving took links through classes to zero or one on PROV-O, telecom, the loan ontology and the 179-class bank, with crossings about level.
  2. From intuition to rules. The assistant turned each principle into something measurable: open space became a grid of empty cells, a crowd became the cells where problems pile up, "make room" became a push weighted by the room beyond each slice. Each rule was implemented and benchmarked on all the test graphs, and kept only if it helped across them. An early version came within a link or two of the hand-tidied drawings on every graph and beat them on several, but took 1 to 8 seconds.
  3. A walk-through to critique. The clean-up can replay its own steps in the Lab, one stage at a time, drawing the open-space grid, the problem regions, the slices and the moved classes. The person critiqued each step the way a teacher marks a student's work ("why didn't the leaf go to the right, where there was room?"), and those critiques became the next rules.
  4. Plain words both ways. The person described strategies in their own terms (grid, density, open space, crowd, pizza slices, center of gravity). The assistant turned them into the definitions in the tabs above, and explained back, in the same plain terms, where a rule was slow or did not work. One example: "a push always looks worse at first; only the repairs after it make it pay off." The person then refined the principle.

Profiling the first version showed that the group moves took about 95% of its time and kept about one try in twenty. Two changes made the clean-up fast enough for the layouts: candidate spots come from the open-space grid (a few dozen instead of 96 fixed positions), and a class or problem that could not be improved is not tried again until something near it has moved.

What is new here, and what is not

Most single ingredients have precedents. What we have not seen elsewhere is the combination: repairs chosen and judged by readability counts after a layout, the regions picked by where those counts pile up, and the steps distilled from a person's explained problem solving. Readers may know closer prior work; we would welcome pointers.

  • Moving one class to a better spot to remove crossings is an established heuristic: Radermacher, Reichard, Rutter and Wagner (2018) move a vertex into its crossing-minimal region. The single moves here sample open cells instead of computing regions, and count links through classes and angles as well as crossings.
  • Turning what hangs off one class. Cut vertices and the parts of a graph they separate are classical (Hopcroft and Tarjan, 1973), and a tree hanging off one vertex can always be drawn without crossings. Using this as a repair after a layout, with the label-aware choice of side, is ours.
  • Spreading a region in slices. A push outward from a point that fades with distance resembles a local fisheye distortion (Sarkar and Brown, 1992), and evening out density resembles density-equalizing maps (Gastner and Newman, 2004). We have not seen it used this way: the point chosen from a count of readability problems after the layout, each slice weighted by the open space beyond it, and the push kept only if a few repair moves then make the drawing better.
  • Judged as graph drawing theory, nothing here is new: no new model, no bounds. The building blocks were chosen because they are the established best for the job, not as stand-ins for something to write from scratch. Stress placement is the standard for general undirected drawings and Pivot MDS its usual start. The new parts are applied: the clean-up and its passes, the routing joined to the placement, the work clock, and the evaluation against eleven libraries on graphs never seen during development.
  • Learning the procedure from a person. Interactive optimization with user hints is established (do Nascimento and Eades, 2005), but there the person guides one run. Here the person's principles, taught once, became a fixed procedure that runs without them on graphs they never saw.

Link routing after the layout

Added on 2026-11-07, after a review that pointed out three gaps for business ontologies: parallel and reciprocal links (Organization employs Person, Person manages Organization) drawn as straight lines lie exactly on top of each other; link names are not part of the drawing; and moving classes is the only way the layouts clear a link out of a class's way. The routing draws the links on the final positions and never moves a class:

  • Parallel links between the same two classes become arcs spread 24 px apart on both sides of the straight line (the middle one straight when their number is odd), so each one and its name can be seen.
  • Self-links become a loop on the corner of the class where the fewest links leave.
  • Bends: a link that runs through a class (or, when it pays off, crosses others) tries one bend at the corners and sides of the boxes in its way, gentle arcs to either side, and pairs of those bends; a bend is kept only if the drawing gets better counting crossings, links through classes and the extra length. Links through classes are routed first, then a second pass for any still in the way.
  • Ports: at a class with three or more links, link ends that would meet its box closer than 10 px apart are spread along the side they reach, in the same order, kept only if no link gets a new problem.
  • Names: each link's name goes at the point along its path where it covers no class, no other name and as few links as possible, longest names first.

Measured on the drawn curves, on Best Read's drawings as recorded on 2026-11-07 (no class moved). Two margins. Here and in every table about the routing, a link counts as running through a class when it passes within 9 px of its box: closer than that, a drawn line with its arrowhead reads as touching the box, and the routing keeps that clearance. The rankings below count within 3 px, as published comparisons of layouts do, so the same drawing has more links through classes here. That, and the earlier drawings, is why Best Read's straight links show 61 links through classes on the 15 development graphs in this table and 39 in the rankings. Changes on 2026-11-08

From watching the playback: loops took a fixed corner even where links left the class, names sat on top of the line, a bend could swing far outside the drawing, and some one-link classes ended fenced in by lines with open space nearby. Changes, each kept only where it measured better:

  • Loops on the open side. A self-link's loop now tries 12 places (four corners, and the middle and both ends of each side) and takes the one with the fewest links, classes and crossings in its wedge, counting the class's own links. On the five graphs with a self-link, four loops crossed a link before; none does now.
  • Names beside the line. A name can also sit just beside its link, on either side, where that covers less. Names on a class or another name 331 → 147, names with a link across them 1,330 → 882.
  • Bends stay inside the drawing. A bend that takes a link outside the box round the classes costs 1 per 20 px. Over 52 graphs, link length outside that box 2,817 → 1,056 px, for 15 more crossings (+0.8%), 3 fewer links through classes and 1.5% less area.
  • Classes moved off links. After the clean-up, three short passes (at most 120 ms of the work clock): two one-link classes of the same class trade places when that leaves fewer problems; a one-link class whose link still crosses or runs through something moves to the most open spot round its neighbor (fewest problems, then the most room, then the shorter link); a class that a link runs through, or passes within 9 px of, is pushed sideways off the line just far enough. After the routing, a class that a drawn curve still runs through is pushed off it the same way, the links are routed again, and the result is kept only if it has fewer problems. On straight links, links through classes 216 → 178.
  • Time limit. _ogSetTimeLimit(ms) sets the time the layouts, the clean-up and the routing may take, as a multiple of the default 1.5 s on the work clock (so still the same drawing on any computer).

Trading two one-link classes' places alone rarely helps: where two of them lie on one ray from their neighbor, the far one's link runs through the near one whichever is farther.

How the layouts work

Steps both layouts share

  1. Target distances. Every link gets a target length (170 px by default; subclass links slightly shorter; a link with a label is long enough for the label). The target distance between any two classes is the length of the shortest path of links between them.
  2. Placement. Classes are placed so that straight-line distances match the target distances as closely as possible (stress majorization), starting from a pivot-MDS placement, which gives the same result every time. Overlapping boxes are then pushed apart.
  3. Tidy-up. Four local searches run in this order. Each keeps a move only if it adds no crossing, no link through a box and no overlap:
    1. Swap two classes, or move one beside a neighbor, when that reduces crossings.
    2. Move a class off links that run through it.
    3. Pull classes toward their neighbors to shorten long links, then shrink the whole drawing evenly as far as the boxes allow.
    4. Move classes off links once more.
    Without these steps (placement alone, as in Graphviz neato) a drawing has typically 2–3× as many links through classes and about twice as many crossings.
  4. Leaf fanning, when used. Classes linked to a single other class are left out of placement and put back last.
    • Each is placed around its neighbor by trying 48 directions at the nearest free distances.
    • The chosen spot minimizes 100 × crossings + 80 × links touching the box + distance.
    • Each keeps at least max(40 px, label width + 16 px) from its neighbor, so the link's label fits.
    • Any whose link ends up crossed or touched is placed again.
    Ontologies have many classes of this kind, such as leaf subclasses.
  5. Clean-up. The drawing chosen is cleaned up as described under Clean-up after the layout.

Tidy Stress

Tidy Stress tries four settings of the shared steps within 1.5 seconds, scores each drawing, and keeps the most readable. The settings differ only in their options, not in the order of the steps:

  • random starts instead of the pivot-MDS start, keeping the start with the fewest crossings;
  • the pivot-MDS start, then moving any single-link class to a better free spot if there is one;
  • leaf fanning, with extra room round classes that have many links (the hub rings described below);
  • leaf fanning alone.

From 31 to 60 classes it tries three of these, and above 60 only the second, because sharing the time between settings leaves each too little time to tidy up.

Mycelium

  • Pods. Louvain community detection finds groups of closely linked classes. Links inside a group get 0.85× the target length and links between groups 1.6×, so placement separates the groups while still letting a class shared by two groups sit between them.
  • Hub rings. A link at a class with k neighbors is at least Σ(neighbor width + 20 px) / 2π long, the radius of a ring that holds all the neighbors side by side. On the clinical-record graph this rule alone cuts links through classes from about 80 to about 43.
  • Leaf fanning, as above.
  • Target length. 260 px from 100 classes.
  • Limits. It needs at least 8 classes and has a 1 s budget.

How readability is scored

Tidy Stress (between its settings) and Best Read (between the two layouts) choose the drawing with the lowest score. This score is how the layouts choose; it is not used to rank layouts on this page, because it has terms for link length. The score adds up:

  • 10 for every pair of crossed links;
  • 8 for every link that runs through a class box;
  • 2 for every pair of links that come within 30 px of each other;
  • 40 for every pair of overlapping boxes;
  • a tenth of the mean link length, plus a penalty when the mean exceeds 350 px;
  • a penalty for area beyond eight times the total area of the boxes, growing with the number of classes;
  • 25 for every multiple, beyond 1.8, of the median link length by which a link is longer.

The last two terms matter. A layout can avoid crossings by pushing classes far apart or by stretching a few links across the whole drawing. Without those terms, such drawings would win on crossings alone even though they are hard to read.

Best Read

  • Up to 30 classes: runs Tidy Stress's four settings and Mycelium, and keeps the lowest score.
  • 31 to 100 classes: runs Tidy Stress's settings for that size and Mycelium. For dense ontologies (more than two links per class) it runs only one Tidy Stress setting, so Mycelium has enough time.
  • Above 100 classes: runs Mycelium alone.

Its budget is 1.5 s up to 30 classes, 2.2 s up to 100, and 1 s above.

Benchmark method

Development graphs. The layouts and the clean-up were developed and tuned on 15 graphs of 13 to 179 classes; four larger ones (120 to 537 classes) were used for timing and scale. Earlier versions of this write-up used 19 graphs, including an HR and a supply-chain ontology.

  • Ontologies written from the published structure of PROV-O, SOSA/SSN, W3C ORG, the Pizza tutorial ontology and a FIBO-like loan ontology.
  • Ontologies for typical domains: university, hotel booking, clinical trial, telecom network, customer 360, schema.org-style core types, a clinical record with three super-hubs, a 115-class biomedical taxonomy and a 179-class bank.
  • For scale: scale-free graphs of 120, 250 and 400 classes, and three banks joined (537 classes).

Graphs never seen during development. Two sets were added after the layouts were finished and were only ever measured, never viewed while tuning:

  • Ten held-out graphs of 18 to 304 classes, generated from fixed seeds in shapes the development graphs do not cover: sparse Rome-style graphs, dense links, process loops, two big hubs, multi-domain models and a deep taxonomy.
  • 26 public benchmark graphs. Eight published ontologies (FOAF, Dublin Core terms, OWL-Time, DCAT 3, BFO 2020, DOLCE+DnS Ultralite, SAREF core and GoodRelations, 13 to 98 classes), drawn as their named classes with subclass links and a link from domain to range for each object property; and 18 graphs of the Rome collection, the classic graph-drawing benchmark (two per size from 20 to 100 nodes, the lowest graph numbers, so the choice involves no judgment).

Boxes. Sized from the label length.

Contenders. Graphviz neato, fdp and sfdp (with Graphviz's own overlap removal), ELK stress, force and layered, WebCola, d3-force, dagre, and Cytoscape cose and fcose. The general-settings ranking used sensible general settings.

Tuned settings. For the comparison against tuned settings, each library kept the best of its default run and these, by fewest crossed links plus links through classes:

  • Graphviz neato: stress majorization, stochastic gradient descent (mode=sgd) and model=subset, two seeds each. sfdp: default, K=1.4, repulsiveforce=2 and smoothing=spring, two seeds each. fdp: K=1.4, three seeds.
  • ELK stress: target lengths 120, 170 and 230 px, 1,000 iterations. ELK force: three seeds, 1,000 iterations. ELK layered: three node-placement strategies, higher thoroughness, subclass links parent to child.
  • dagre: three ranking methods, subclass links parent to child.
  • WebCola: three seeds. d3-force: three seeds × three repulsion strengths. Cytoscape cose: three seeds × two repulsion strengths. fcose: three seeds × three repulsion strengths.

On the 179-class bank, tuned ELK force gave no result within two minutes, so its default run stands in.

Measures. Crossed links: pairs of straight links without a shared end that intersect. Links through classes: a link passing within 3 px of a third class box (the tables on the link routing use 9 px, see there). Area: the bounding box divided by the total area of the class boxes. Crossing angle: the mean angle at which links cross (Huang, Eades and Hong, 2008). Angular resolution: the smallest angle between the links at a class as a share of 360° divided by its number of links. Normalized stress (distance fidelity), the variation of link lengths and the share of subclass links drawn top-down were also recorded; they are left out of the main rankings for the reasons given at the top, and each ranking table has tabs that add them.

Fairness. Every layout received the same boxes, the same 170 px target link length where it takes one, and the same overlap removal afterwards.

Runs. One run per layout and graph, seed 1, on one consumer laptop, so the times compare with each other but are not absolute. The open-source layouts ran in a browser tab; ours ran at native speed in Node.js with the same code the library runs, which takes about as long as a browser tab in front. Our time limits count work rather than seconds, so our drawings do not depend on the machine or how busy it was.

Experiments: top-down hierarchy and bent links

Reviews of earlier versions of this write-up raised two design questions: whether subclass links should point down, and whether links should bend around classes instead of classes moving off links.

Subclass links drawn top-down

What these layouts are for. Tidy Stress, Mycelium and Best Read are built for conceptual ontologies, where classes are joined mostly by relationships to other, non-hierarchical classes: a data model, a domain model, an integration ontology. There the reader's task is following relationships, and a subclass link is one relationship among many; these layouts treat it like any other link, and about half point down. For a taxonomy, a model made mostly of subclass links, the hierarchy is the content, and a reader rightly expects general classes above specific ones. A reviewer called the first stance a semantic defect; for taxonomies we agree, and the layouts now handle the two cases differently.

  • Best Read drew a taxonomy as a tree when that cost nothing (until 2026-11-07; dropped since, see Best Read). When at least half the links are subclass links, it also lays the model out as a top-down tree and keeps the tree if the tree has no crossed link and no link through a class, in at most 1.5 times the area of the best other drawing. The clean-up then keeps every subclass below its superclass. On the graphs never seen during development this chose the tree for Dublin Core terms, with no crossing, no link through a class and every subclass link pointing down. For BFO 2020 the tree is just as clean but about 1.55 times the area of the other drawing, which has no problem either. It changed nothing on the 15 development graphs, where a top-down drawing is never free.
  • The Tree layout, chosen on its own, is this tree for any model, followed by the clean-up, which keeps it top-down. In the tree each class hangs under its shallowest superclass, siblings sit side by side, and each row of children sits deep enough below its parent that no line from the parent to a child clips a sibling, so the tree itself draws no crossing and no link through a class. Only second superclasses and relationships can cross.
  • When the hierarchy costs. Taxonomies with many cross-links pay for top-down order, whichever layout draws them. Counted as in the Layout Lab, after the clean-up:
    • Pizza (90 classes, 95% subclass links): the tree draws 7 crossed links and none through classes, but six times wider than Best Read's drawing, which has 3 and 6 with half the subclass links pointing down. Tuned ELK layered draws 2 and 41 with its straight links measured, tuned dagre 24 and 164.
    • Biomedical taxonomy (115 classes, 82% subclass links): the tree draws 114 and 17, Best Read 25 and 0, tuned ELK layered 76 and 44, tuned dagre 157 and 181.
    So for a taxonomy with many cross-links, the Tree layout gives the most readable top-down drawing we found, and Best Read gives fewer problems by letting direction go.
  • A soft hierarchy term in the placement, as the review suggested (a penalty λ·max(0, yparent − ychild + δ)² added to stress, in the spirit of Dwyer and Koren's DIG-CoLa, 2005), was tested two ways. As a partial correction after each placement sweep, on eight development graphs before the clean-up, it turned 94% of subclass links down but raised crossed links from 203 to 262 and links through classes from 91 to 166. As a true term of the stress energy, minimized by the same majorization (each violated subclass link adds a target height to its class's update, as DIG-CoLa does), it hardly changed direction: even at λ = 16, over the 15 graphs, the share pointing down rose only from 46% to 54%, with 14% more links through classes, because the tidy-up and the placement of one-link classes that follow decide most directions, and making them keep the hierarchy is the hard version measured below. Reflecting a child below its parent instead of pressing it flat, so that a parent's children keep their fan, did no better (332 and 190). In the clean-up, keeping every subclass at least 20 px below raised crossed links 27% and links through classes fourfold over the 15 graphs; even as a small tie-breaker it raised links through classes from 55 to 67.

Readers who value a top-down hierarchy can also rank by it: the ranking tables have a tab that adds it as a sixth measure. The rest of this section is the earlier experiment, run before the clean-up existed, so its numbers are without it. The variant tested adds a separation constraint for every subclass link, subclass at least 40 px below its parent, in the spirit of Dwyer, Koren and Marriott's constrained stress majorization (IPSep-CoLa, 2006). The constraints are applied by projection after each placement sweep, and every tidy-up move and leaf placement must keep them.

Bending links instead of moving classes

The layouts and the clean-up remove links through classes by moving classes, which costs area. The alternative, standard in yFiles, MSAGL and ELK, is to leave the classes where the placement put them and route each offending link around the classes in its way. We tested a simple router: one or two bends at the padded corners of the boxes in the way, choosing the bends that cross the fewest other links (a full obstacle-avoiding router would do better). Rerun with the current layouts, averages per graph over the 15 development graphs:

The review is right that routing is a real alternative: placement plus bends draws a fifth fewer crossings in 9% less area, at about 18 bends per graph and twice the links through classes left over. Moving classes keeps every link straight. The cheapest gain is the third row: two bends per graph remove almost every link through a class that the moves leave. This became the link routing after the layout (see Link routing), with arcs for parallel links, ports and name placement added. Whether a bend costs a reader less than a crossing is a question for a study; Purchase's studies rank bends as less harmful than crossings.

Limits

  • Which measures count. The main rankings leave out distance fidelity and even link lengths on purpose; a tab on each ranking table adds them, and our layouts rank lower with them in. No study with people reading the diagrams has been done, so which measures matter most for understanding an ontology is a judgment, not a finding.
  • Measuring what we optimize. The layouts and the clean-up try to reduce crossed links and links through classes, and the rankings count them, so a lead on those two is partly by design. Three things keep this honest: crossing angle and angular resolution are ranked but not optimized by the clean-up beyond avoiding sharp crossings; the tuned open-source runs were chosen by the same two counts, so each library was given its best chance on exactly what we are judged on; and the results on graphs never seen during development show the lead is not fitted to the 15 development graphs.
  • Tuning. The tuned settings are a reasonable search, not an exhaustive one. A library tuned per graph by an expert could do better.
  • Straight links in the rankings. Every drawing in the rankings is measured with straight links, as the layouts place them; the link routing that now runs after the layout (see Link routing) is measured separately, on our drawings only. ELK layered can route its own links around boxes, so it was also measured that way, on the links it routes itself (best of four placement settings and two routings per graph, over the 15 development graphs): no link runs through a class, but 1,322 pairs of links cross, against 450 for Best Read with straight links, with about 165 bends per graph. Bends are not a defect in themselves: routing links around boxes is a different trade-off, keeping classes in place and adding bends, and our own experiment with it is under Experiments. A comparison with a dedicated obstacle-avoiding router, as in yFiles or MSAGL, has not been done.
  • Hierarchy. Tidy Stress and Mycelium do not draw subclass links top-down; about half point down. Best Read no longer switches to a top-down tree; the Tree layout is there for taxonomies where the hierarchy matters more than the last few crossings. See Experiments. Consistent upward or downward flow is a standard aesthetic for directed graphs in the graph-drawing literature; our choice for conceptual ontologies is a judgment, not a finding.
  • No study with people. The rankings use geometric measures only. A task-based study (find the common superclass of two classes, name a relationship's domain and range), timed and scored against tuned Graphviz, WebCola and a commercial engine with orthogonal routing, would settle which drawings read best; it has not been done.
  • Test graphs. The 15 development graphs are reconstructions and inventions of typical shapes. The graphs never seen during development add eight published ontologies and 18 Rome graphs, but not a large corpus such as the OBO Foundry or the Manchester OWL corpus, and the ontologies were drawn as classes with subclass and domain-to-range links only. Commercial engines (yFiles, Tom Sawyer) were not tested.
  • Very large graphs. Best Read leads up to the 1,204-class graph tested; at 3,335 classes d3-force draws far fewer problems (see Graphs of over a thousand classes). Models of thousands of classes need a multilevel placement, which these layouts do not have. At 537 classes, tuned WebCola matches Mycelium's crossings (2,005 against 2,012), with more links through classes, after three minutes against Mycelium's one second.
  • Greedy local search and hand-set weights. The tidy-up and the clean-up keep only moves that improve the drawing, so they stop at local optima, and their weights (a crossed link costs 6, a link through a class 10) were set by benchmark on the development graphs, not derived. The unseen graphs show the settings carry over; they are not shown to be optimal. Two tests since: with every class name three times as long (15 development graphs) and on dense random graphs (40 to 150 classes, 6 to 10 links per class), every run finished within its time limit, gave the same drawing twice, and the clean-up still improved the drawing by its own weights. With long names it trades crossings for links through classes: 439 → 499 crossed links, 107 → 35 links through classes. On the dense graphs it reduced both in total (crossed links 27,920 → 25,600, links through classes 2,874 → 2,250). Turning or mirroring a whole branch round the one class that joins it to the rest (the move a greedy search cannot make one class at a time) was tried; see What did not work.
  • The clean-up was taught by one person. Its principles come from one expert, shown on eight ontologies. Another reader may tidy differently, and the ideas not yet built in (curving links around hubs, tightening loops) may matter more elsewhere.
  • Seed-to-seed variation. The time limits count work rather than seconds, so a model gets the same drawing on any computer up to 2.5 times slower than the one the layouts were tuned on. The layouts do use a random seed, and different seeds give different drawings: over five seeds, totals over the 15 graphs differ by a few percent. Every result on this page uses seed 1.
  • Hub-heavy graphs stay hard. On the clinical-record graph, a 20-second exhaustive search reaches only about 150 crossings, against Mycelium's 164 with the clean-up. Large further gains need a different kind of drawing, such as bundled links or showing a hub more than once.

What did not work

  • Stretching the drawing around a class until every single-link neighbor has a clean spot. Without a limit it produced drawings millions of pixels wide on hub-heavy graphs; with a limit of 2.5× it doubled the area for little gain.
  • Pulling hubs toward the center. No gain.
  • Other placement weights. 1/d in place of 1/d². No gain.
  • Other link lengths. Lengths scaled by the number of links or by shared neighbors. No gain.
  • Other ways of finding pods:
    • overlapping groups, where a class also joins any group that holds 30% of its links;
    • coarser and finer group sizes.
    Each did better on some graphs and worse on others, with no gain on average. The pods only change link lengths, so having groups at all matters more than exactly how they are drawn. Removing them costs most on large graphs: on the bank, crossings rise from 73 to 128 and links through classes from 53 to 103.
  • Sharing a fixed time budget among several settings on large graphs. Each setting gets too little time to tidy up; one setting with all the time does better.
  • Clean-up: spreading every dense region. Spreading regions by density alone, problems or not, was never kept: it disturbed parts of the drawing that already read well.
  • Clean-up: judging a spread on its own. Without repair moves after it, no spread was ever kept, because pushing classes apart first adds crossings at the edge of the region.
  • Clean-up: regions that grow without limit. When every cell with a problem joined a region, the region covered the whole drawing and the spread became a zoom of everything. Regions are now only the hottest cells, at most one and a half link lengths across.
  • Turning or mirroring whole branches. A branch joined to the rest through one class only (by any number of links), turned round that class or mirrored, kept when its problems drop. It found a better place on 4 of 64 graphs and changed nothing in total over 51 graphs and three seeds (5,199 against 5,202 crossed links): the stress placement and the leaf-unit moves already leave no branch on the wrong side of its hub.
  • Clean-up: turning larger groups. Turning the parts hanging off a class as one piece, up to 12 classes, made no measurable difference over the limit of 4.

Source code

The implementation below is complete and self-contained: plain JavaScript, no dependencies, about 1,800 lines, the clean-up included.

Input. An array of boxes [{ w, h }] and an array of links [[i, j, weight, labelWidth]]:

  • i and j index into the boxes;
  • weight is 2 for subclass links and 1 for others;
  • labelWidth, the pixel width of the link's label, is optional.

Output. An object whose pos array holds the center { x, y } of each box. All arithmetic is on unrounded floating-point numbers.

const boxes = [{ w: 90, h: 20 }, { w: 70, h: 20 }, { w: 110, h: 20 }];
const links = [[1, 0, 2], [2, 0, 1, 60]];       // 1 is a subclass of 0; 2 links to 0 with a 60 px label
// Everything in one call: layout, clean-up, link routing, groups, and paths ready to draw.
const d = OntologyLayouts.draw(boxes, links, { arrow: 8 });
d.pos.forEach((p, i) => console.log(i, p.x, p.y));        // draw class i centered here
d.paths.forEach((p) => console.log(p.points, p.tip, p.angle)); // border to border; an 8 px arrowhead from the last point to tip
console.log(d.groups);                                      // a group number per class, for hulls or background areas
// The same in three steps. Time is read from the work clock, OntologyLayouts.now(), not from the computer's clock,
// so the same model gets the same drawing on any computer:
const t0 = OntologyLayouts.now();
const { pos } = OntologyLayouts.bestRead(boxes, links, {});    // or .tidyStress(…) / .mycelium(…)
const cleanPos = OntologyLayouts.cleanUp(pos, boxes, links, t0); // within what is left of 1.5 s since t0
const { routes, pos: finalPos } = OntologyLayouts.routeLinks(cleanPos, boxes, links);
// finalPos, not cleanPos: the routing may push a class off a link it still runs through, and the routes belong to finalPos.
const paths = OntologyLayouts.routePaths(finalPos, boxes, links, routes, { arrow: 8 });
// options: bestRead(…, { roomy: true }) leaves 30% more space; routeLinks(…, { settle: false }) never moves a class;
// OntologyLayouts.setTimeLimit(ms) sets the time for all three steps together (default 1500).
// After an edit: pass the earlier drawing's positions (null for each new class) to keep the reader's picture.
const d2 = OntologyLayouts.draw(boxes2, links2, { keep: oldPos });               // old classes move a few percent
const d3 = OntologyLayouts.draw(boxes2, links2, { keep: oldPos, exact: true });  // old classes do not move at all
// A relationship modelled as a class (reified, or an association class): mark it, and it is drawn on the line between the two.
boxes2[7].between = [2, 5];                                                       // class 7 relates class 2 to class 5
// Mycelium returns null for fewer than 8 classes; Best Read then uses Tidy Stress.

The entry points are collected at the end of the code in OntologyLayouts: draw, bestRead, tidyStress, mycelium, cleanUp, routeLinks, routePaths, groups, routeMetrics, metrics, score, setTimeLimit, now, placeNew and inline.

Keeping the picture after an edit. Redrawing a model after an edit moves the classes a reader already knows: with 10% more classes, a redraw moves the old ones by about a third of the drawing's diagonal on average (25 graphs). draw(…, { keep }) takes the earlier positions, with null for each new class. It places the new classes round the classes they link to, then lets the clean-up and the routing make local moves. With exact: true no old class moves at all, which suits a drawing arranged by hand. Keeping costs crossings, because the new links have to cross a picture that was not made for them; redrawing from time to time remains the way to a clean drawing. These numbers are the hard case: the added classes were picked at random, hubs included, where an edit usually adds a few classes in one place.

Relation classes. A relationship modelled as a class (RDF reification, an association class, an n-ary relation pattern) reads best on the line between the two classes it relates. Give its box between: [subject, object], and after the clean-up it moves onto that line, as near the middle as there is room, when that costs it at most one crossing. On the 15 development graphs with every third relationship made a class (225 relation classes): relation classes bent by more than 45 degrees 100 → 66, mean angle at the relation class 129° → 147°, crossed links 521 → 507, links through classes 16 → 12. Without the hint a relation class is an ordinary class, because which classes are relationships is a modelling decision the links alone do not show.

Draw the classes where the routing leaves them. routeLinks returns pos as well as the routes: when a drawn link still runs through a class, the class is pushed off it and the links are routed again, and the result is kept only if it has fewer problems. Those positions are the final ones. A renderer that keeps the earlier positions draws routes that miss their classes. Pass { settle: false } to keep every class where it is.

Arrowheads. routePaths turns each route into points from the border of one box to the border of the other. With arrow, each path stops that many pixels short of its end class along its last direction, so an arrowhead drawn from there to tip, at angle, touches the box without entering it. Spreading the arrow ends further apart on a shared side was tried and not kept: on the 15 development graphs it changed no drawing. Where arrowheads crowd, it is at a hub with more incoming links than its side has room for, and spacing them further adds crossings.

Groups. groups, and draw's groups, give each class the number of its group of closely linked classes. These are the groups that Mycelium keeps together and the pull-in slides inward, for a renderer that draws hulls, swimlanes or background areas round related classes.

Time limits count work, not seconds. Every time limit (the layouts' and the clean-up's) is measured in units of work: tests of two links, of a link against a box, steps tried, each with a fixed cost in nanoseconds measured on the laptop the layouts were tuned on. A computer half as fast therefore takes twice as long and draws exactly the same picture, instead of drawing a worse one in the same time. To keep the wait bounded on very slow computers, the wall clock still applies at 1/2.5 speed: a computer more than 2.5 times slower gets less work and a somewhat different drawing.
